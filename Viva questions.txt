🔹 J. Model Diagnostics & Assumptions

What assumptions does ARIMA require?
Stationarity (constant mean and variance over time), no autocorrelation in residuals, and normally distributed residuals.

How do you check if residuals are white noise?
By plotting the ACF of residuals and performing the Ljung-Box test. Non-significant autocorrelation indicates white noise.

What does the Ljung-Box test tell you?
It tests whether residuals exhibit significant autocorrelation. A p-value > 0.05 suggests the model adequately captures the data structure.

Why is stationarity important in time-series modeling?
Non-stationary data leads to unreliable parameter estimates and poor forecasts. Differencing or transformation is used to achieve stationarity.

🔹 K. Model Selection & Comparison

What are AIC, BIC, and AICc, and how are they used in model selection?
AIC (Akaike Information Criterion), BIC (Bayesian Information Criterion), and AICc (corrected AIC) are criteria used to compare models by balancing goodness of fit with model complexity. They penalize models with more parameters to avoid overfitting. Lower values indicate better models. AIC is calculated as 2k - 2ln(L), where k is the number of parameters and L is the likelihood. BIC penalizes complexity more heavily than AIC, making it more conservative, and is preferred for larger datasets. AICc adjusts AIC for small sample sizes and is recommended when the ratio of sample size to parameters is low (n/k < 40). Use AIC for prediction-focused selection, BIC when parsimony is critical, and AICc for small datasets.

When should you choose ARIMA vs SARIMA vs Holt-Winters?
ARIMA is appropriate for non-seasonal time series that become stationary after differencing. It models autocorrelation structures through AR and MA components. SARIMA extends ARIMA to handle data with clear seasonal patterns at fixed intervals (e.g., monthly, quarterly) by adding seasonal AR, MA, and differencing terms. Holt-Winters Exponential Smoothing is ideal when data show trend and seasonality, offering additive or multiplicative options depending on whether seasonal variation is constant or proportional to the level. Choose ARIMA when there is no seasonality, SARIMA when seasonal cycles are evident and stable, and Holt-Winters when trend and seasonality are present but the data structure is relatively simple and stable. Model diagnostics and forecast accuracy metrics guide the final choice.

How do you determine the parameters p, d, and q in ARIMA models?
The parameter d (differencing order) is determined by testing for stationarity using the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test or KPSS test. Apply differencing until the series becomes stationary. The parameter q (MA order) is identified from the ACF plot: significant spikes at early lags that cut off after lag q suggest an MA(q) model. The parameter p (AR order) is identified from the PACF plot: significant spikes that cut off after lag p suggest an AR(p) model. For SARIMA models, seasonal parameters P, D, and Q are determined similarly using ACF and PACF plots at seasonal lags (e.g., lags 12, 24 for monthly data). Model selection is iterative—fit candidate models, examine residual diagnostics, and compare AIC/BIC values to choose the best specification.

Why can't you use random train-test splits in time-series forecasting?
Random splitting violates the temporal dependency inherent in time-series data. Forecasting models rely on past observations to predict future values, so training on future data and testing on past data would be invalid and misleading. Proper time-series validation uses a temporal split: the model is trained on earlier data and tested on later, unseen data. A common approach is to reserve the last 10-20% of observations (e.g., the last 12 months) for testing. Rolling-window or expanding-window cross-validation can also be used, where the model is repeatedly trained on growing subsets and validated on the next period. This mimics real-world forecasting scenarios and provides a realistic assessment of model performance on future data.
