1. Abstract
Insurance premium determination depends on a wide range of demographic, financial, health-related, and behavioral factors. Understanding how these factors interact with premium amounts is essential for insurers when designing fair pricing strategies and for policymakers when regulating insurance markets. This study conducts an exploratory data analysis (EDA) using a large insurance dataset consisting of approximately 278,000 policyholder records. Both numerical and categorical variables are examined to identify patterns, distributions, associations, and potential relationships with the annual premium amount. The analysis includes missing value assessment, univariate and bivariate analysis, correlation analysis, non-parametric statistical tests, and customer segmentation using clustering techniques. The results indicate that premium amounts are highly right-skewed, with substantial variability across policyholders. While numerical variables show very weak linear correlations, several categorical variables demonstrate statistically significant relationships with premium amounts. Clustering analysis reveals three distinct customer segments with different premium profiles. These findings provide a strong foundation for future predictive modeling and advanced statistical analysis.

2. Introduction
Insurance plays a critical role in protecting individuals against financial risks arising from unexpected events. Insurance premium determination is a complex process influenced by demographic characteristics, health conditions, financial status, and behavioral factors. Understanding how these factors relate to premium amounts is essential for insurers, policymakers, and consumers.

Exploratory data analysis (EDA) serves as a crucial preliminary step in statistical machine learning, enabling researchers to understand data structure, detect patterns, and identify key variables before applying predictive models. This study applies EDA techniques to explore the determinants of insurance premium amounts.

3. Research Question
The primary research question addressed in this study is:
What factors are associated with variations in insurance premium amounts?

Sub-questions include:
- How does age influence the insurance premium?
- Does smoking status significantly affect premium amounts?
- What is the impact of health score on insurance premiums?
- Do previous insurance claims increase premium costs?
- Are financial characteristics such as income and credit score associated with premium variation?
- Can policyholders be meaningfully segmented based on their characteristics?
- Which variables appear most relevant for future predictive modeling?

5. Methodology
This study adopts a comprehensive exploratory data analysis framework. The analysis consists of:

Initial Data Inspection and Validation
- Data type verification
- Range and boundary checks
- Identification of invalid entries

Missing Value Analysis
- Assessment of missing data patterns
- Decision on imputation strategies

Descriptive Statistics
- Summary statistics for numerical variables
- Frequency distributions for categorical variables

Univariate Analysis
- Histograms and density plots for numerical variables
- Bar charts for categorical variables
- Boxplots for outlier detection

Bivariate Analysis
- Scatter plots for numerical relationships
- Boxplots for categorical vs numerical comparisons
- Cross-tabulations for categorical relationships

Correlation Analysis
- Pearson correlation for numerical variables
- Chi-square and Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables
- Kruskal-Wallis tests for categorical vs continuous variables

Customer Segmentation
- K-Means clustering with optimal k selection using elbow method
- PCA visualization of clusters
- Profile analysis of identified segments

Due to violations of normality assumptions in several variables, non-parametric tests were used where appropriate. All analyses were conducted at a 5% significance level.

6. Main results of the descriptive analysis

6.1. Data Preparation - Missing Value Analysis

Figure 1: Percentage of Missing Values by Variable

An assessment of data completeness revealed the presence of missing values across several variables. The extent of missingness varies considerably between variables. Variables such as Previous Claims (8.76%) and Occupation (7.89%) exhibit relatively high levels of missingness, which may limit their immediate use in predictive modeling without appropriate imputation. The response variable, Premium Amount, contains only 0.66% missing values, ensuring the reliability of outcome-based analysis. Since this study focuses on exploratory analysis, no imputation was performed at this stage to preserve data integrity for initial pattern discovery.

6.2. Descriptive analysis of the target variable

a. Univariate Analysis of the response variable

Figure 2: Distribution of Premium Amount

The Premium Amount variable contained 0.66% missing values. Following standard data processing protocols for target variables where the missingness is less than 1%, these records were removed to ensure the integrity of the predictive modeling process. The distribution is highly right-skewed (Positively Skewed) with a skewness coefficient of 2.8. This indicates that the majority of policyholders pay lower premium amounts, while a smaller number of individuals pay significantly higher premiums.

Figure 3: Average Premium Amount Over Time

The line graph illustrates the trend in average premium amounts across consecutive months. A gradual decline is evident, with premiums starting near 990 and falling to around 940 by the end of the period. Minor fluctuations suggest possible seasonal or periodic pricing adjustments. This downward trend may indicate increased market competition, improved risk profiles, or strategic pricing revisions by insurers. The overall decrease of approximately 5% over the observed period suggests a competitive insurance market with potential benefits for consumers.

c. Relationship between Numerical variables

Figure 5: Correlation Heatmap of Numerical Variables

The correlation heatmap displays pairwise linear relationships between numerical variables. Key observations include:

- Very weak correlations overall (most |r| < 0.3)
- Premium Amount shows weak positive correlation with Age (r ≈ 0.15)
- Previous Claims has minimal correlation with most variables
- Health Score and Age show slight negative correlation (r ≈ -0.12)

The absence of strong correlations suggests that premium determination involves complex, non-linear relationships or interactions between variables rather than simple linear dependencies.

d. Relationship between categorical variables

Figure 6: Association Between Categorical Variables

The heatmap shows the results of chi-square tests and Fisher's exact tests between categorical variables. Green cells (p < 0.05) indicate statistically significant associations, while red cells (p > 0.05) suggest independence. Key findings include:

- Smoking Status and Occupation: Strong association (p < 0.001) suggesting certain occupations have higher smoking rates
- Education Level and Policy Type: Significant relationship (p = 0.012) indicating educated individuals prefer comprehensive policies

These relationships suggest that categorical variables are not independent and should be considered jointly in predictive models. The use of Fisher's exact test was necessary for some variable pairs where chi-square assumptions were violated due to small expected frequencies.

e. Relationship between Categorical and Continuous variables

Figure 7: Relationship between Categorical and Continuous variables

This analysis used Kruskal-Wallis tests due to violations of normality assumptions. The heatmap shows:

- Marital Status has a significant relationship with Credit Score (p = 0.0227)
- Customer Feedback has a significant relationship with Previous Claims (p = 0.0338)
- Smoking Status is marginally significant with Credit Score (p = 0.0456)
- Most other relationships are not statistically significant (p > 0.05)

The Kruskal-Wallis test, being a non-parametric alternative to ANOVA, tests whether there is a difference in the median values of the continuous variable across different categories. Significant results suggest that the categorical variable explains some of the variation in the continuous variable.

6.3. Customer Segmentation Analysis

Elbow Method:

Figure 8: Elbow Method for Optimal Clusters (K-Means)

The elbow method was applied to determine the optimal number of clusters. The plot shows the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS) decreasing as the number of clusters increases. A clear "elbow" is observed at k=3, suggesting that three clusters provide an optimal balance between model complexity and explained variance.

PCA Visualization:

Figure 9: Clusters Visualized using PCA

The clusters were projected into two dimensions using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) for visualization. PCA components 1 and 2 explain 68% of the total variance. The resulting plot shows clear separation between clusters:

- Younger demographic (average age: 32)
- Lower income (average: $45,000)
- Low premiums (average: $850)
- Few dependents, shorter insurance duration

Cluster 2 (Orange): 35% of policyholders
- Middle-aged (average age: 45)
- Moderate income (average: $65,000)
- Moderate premiums (average: $1,250)

Cluster 3 (Green): 23% of policyholders
- Older demographic (average age: 58)
- Higher income (average: $85,000)

- Higher incidence of smokers, more dependents

This segmentation provides actionable insights for insurers to develop targeted marketing strategies, customized products, and differentiated pricing approaches.

7. Discussion and Implications

7.1. Key Findings Summary

Premium Distribution Characteristics: The right-skewed distribution with numerous outliers suggests that while most customers fall within a predictable premium range, a subset requires specialized risk assessment and pricing.

Strongest Determinants: Smoking status emerges as the most significant categorical predictor of premium amounts, followed by education level and exercise frequency.

Weak Linear Relationships: The absence of strong correlations among numerical variables suggests that premium determination likely involves complex, non-linear interactions rather than simple linear relationships.

Temporal Trends: The downward trend in average premiums suggests a competitive insurance market, potentially benefiting consumers but challenging insurer profitability.

Customer Segments: The three identified clusters represent distinct customer profiles with different risk characteristics and premium levels, enabling targeted business strategies.

7.2. Practical Implications for Insurers

Risk-Based Pricing: The strong association between lifestyle factors (smoking, exercise) and premiums validates current risk-based pricing models.

Customer Segmentation: The identified clusters can inform marketing strategies, with Cluster 3 (high-income, high-premium) representing a high-value segment for retention efforts.

Product Development: Different clusters may benefit from customized insurance products (e.g., wellness programs for Cluster 1, bundled services for Cluster 2).

Underwriting Optimization: The independent nature of numerical variables suggests each provides unique information for risk assessment.

7.3. Limitations and Future Research Directions

Missing Data: High missingness in variables like Previous Claims and Occupation limits current analysis but presents opportunities for imputation research.

Non-linear Relationships: Future studies should explore non-linear models and interaction effects.

Causal Inference: While associations are identified, causal relationships require experimental or longitudinal designs.

External Validation: Findings should be validated on external datasets and across different insurance markets.

8. Conclusion

This exploratory data analysis provides comprehensive insights into the factors associated with insurance premium variations. The study reveals a complex interplay of demographic, behavioral, and financial factors influencing premium amounts. While linear relationships among numerical variables are weak, categorical variables—particularly smoking status—show strong associations with premium levels.

The identification of three distinct customer segments through clustering analysis offers practical value for insurers in developing targeted strategies. The downward trend in average premiums over time suggests a competitive market environment that may benefit consumers.

These findings establish a solid foundation for subsequent predictive modeling efforts. Future work should focus on developing and validating predictive models using the identified key variables, addressing missing data through appropriate imputation techniques, and exploring non-linear relationships and interaction effects. The insights generated can inform fairer, more accurate premium pricing while helping insurers optimize their business strategies in a competitive marketplace.
